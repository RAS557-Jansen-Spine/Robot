RAS 557: FOLDABLE ROBOTICSProject: Bio-Inspired Myriapod Robot

Analysis of a Bio-Inspired Myriapod Robot with a Modified Spine and Legs Linkage

Jeevan Hebbal Manjunath • Varun Karthik • Yeshwanth Reddy Gurreddy

Arizona State University


Download Full Report (PDF)

Abstract

This study investigates the locomotory dynamics of a bio-inspired segmented quadruped robot, focusing on the coupling between spinal compliance and complex leg kinematics. While prior centimeter-scale myriapod robots typically employ simple rotational legs and uniform backbones, this work proposes a hybrid morphology that combines a rigid anterior torso interface with a compliant, Sarrus-inspired parallel articulated spine, driven by four single-degree-of-freedom Jansen leg mechanisms.

A multi-body dynamic model is developed in MuJoCo to simulate the nonlinear interaction between ground reaction forces generated by the Jansen linkages and the restoring forces of the modified spine linkage. A parametric sweep over spinal stiffness (k) and inter-segment length (L) is used to identify configurations that maximize forward velocity and stride frequency while minimizing lateral body oscillations. The resulting design is implemented using the Smart Composite Microstructures (SCM) process, with laser-machined rigid links and multi-layer polymer flexures forming both the spine and leg subsystems.

Experimental trials are compared against simulation predictions to quantify the simulation-to-reality gap, specifically isolating the effects of unmodeled joint friction in the laminate joints. Results demonstrate that appropriately tuning spinal stiffness enables passive regulation of gait dynamics and improves stability, suggesting a general design strategy for high-DOF segmented robots that avoids reliance on complex active control.

Introduction

The development of agile terrestrial robots capable of navigating unstructured environments remains a significant engineering challenge. Bio-inspired systems offer a robust alternative by leveraging "morphological intelligence"—the physical encoding of control strategies into the mechanical structure itself. Specifically, arthropods such as myriapods (e.g., Lithobius forficatus or stone centipede) utilize segmented body plans coupled with compliant inter-connections to decouple body posture from ground reaction forces. This architecture permits stable locomotion over uneven terrain through passive mechanical adaptation rather than complex active control.

In this study, we translate these biological principles into a meso-scale, foldable robotic platform using the Smart Composite Microstructures (SCM) manufacturing process.

Motivation and Project Goals

The primary motivation of this work is to quantify the dynamic trade-offs inherent in compliant segmented locomotion. While hexapedal (6-legged) implementations benefit from inherently stable tripod gaits, they often mask the specific contribution of spinal compliance to stability. By reducing the morphology to a quadruped (4-legged) system, we create a more challenging dynamic environment where the spine's role in maintaining ground contact becomes critical.

  • Synthesize a High-Clearance Morphology: Integrate single-degree-of-freedom Jansen linkages to generate foot trajectories with superior vertical clearance compared to simple rotary legs.
  • Design a Novel Compliant Spine: Replace complex multi-stage backbones with a simplified Cross-Axis Flexure (X-Flexure) spine, designed to provide tunable lateral flexibility while maintaining torsional rigidity.
  • Bridge Simulation and Reality: Formulate a physics-based model in MuJoCo to predict the gait dynamics of this quadrupedal system and validate these predictions against a physical SCM prototype.
  • Optimize Passive Dynamics: Investigate how the stiffness of the X-flexure spine influences the robot's ability to maintain a stable trot gait without complex active feedback.

Biological Inspiration: From Centipede to Robot

Our design is biologically grounded in the morphology of myriapods. Although we have simplified the system to four legs, we retain the core biological principles that define myriapod locomotion:

  • Segmentation and Articulation: Myriapods are not rigid beams; they are chains of articulated units. Our two-segment design mimics a single "vertebral" interface.
  • Distributed Propulsion: Locomotion is driven by the coordinated movement of legs on distinct segments.
  • Passive Adaptation: The inherent compliance of the exoskeleton allows the body to conform to obstacles passively, reducing the computational burden on the central nervous system.

The direct engineering lineage traces back to the myriapod microrobot architecture by Hoffman and Wood [1]. However, we introduced major deviations:

  1. Morphology Reduction (6 Legs -> 4 Legs): To isolate spinal dynamics without tripod stability crutches.
  2. Spine Evolution (Sarrus -> Cross-Axis Flexure): For better lateral bending while preventing sagging.
  3. Leg Mechanism (4-Bar -> Jansen): For deterministic semi-ovoid foot loci and higher lift.

Robot Architecture & Design

Baseline Template & Modifications

The baseline template follows the myriapod concept: a central body with repeated leg modules and a compliant backbone. We retain the overall idea but replace the Sarrus linkage with a simpler laminated wedge spine while reducing the number of legs. This allows us to focus on the interaction between leg propulsion and spine stiffness.

Jansen Linkage Locomotion

To achieve effective ground clearance and a deterministic foot path, we implemented the Jansen linkage. Unlike simple rotary legs, this mechanism converts a simple rotary input into a complex semi-ovoid trajectory with a distinct stance (traction) and swing (lift) phase.

The kinematics of the foot position $\mathbf{p}_f$ are a function of the input crank angle $\theta_{crank}$ and the geometric link lengths $\mathbf{l}$:

$$ \mathbf{p}_f(\theta_{crank}) = \mathcal{F}_{Jansen}(\theta_{crank}, \mathbf{l}) $$

This linkage was selected because its high step height reduces the probability of "toe-stubbing" on carpeted or uneven surfaces, a common failure mode in low-profile crawlers.

Parallel Articulation Mechanism (PAM) Spine

The core innovation of this platform is the variable-geometry spine. Unlike a monolithic flexible beam, our spine is constructed as a discrete linkage linkage (Cross-Axis Flexure).

Variable Stiffness ($k$)

The spine stiffness is governed by the geometry of the flexure hinges and the material properties of the laminate layers. By varying the width of the castellated flexures, we can tune the torsional spring constant $k_{\theta}$.

$$ \tau_{restore} = -k_{\theta} (\theta_{yaw}) - c \dot{\theta}_{yaw} $$

where $\tau_{restore}$ is the restoring torque helping the robot return to neutral heading after a step cycle.

Variable Length ($L$)

The spine design allows for adjustment of the longitudinal distance ($L$) between front and rear axes. This influences the robot's turning moment of inertia $I_z$:

$$ I_z \approx I_{cm} + m \left(\frac{L}{2}\right)^2 $$

Optimizing $L$ is a trade-off: a longer spine increases stability (wheelbase) but increases drag moment during turning.

Fabrication Process

Smart Composite Microstructures (SCM)

We utilized a 5-layer SCM laminate structure to create lightweight, monolithic kinematic chains. The stack-up consists of:

  1. Rigid: Cardstock/Posterboard (Structural base)
  2. Adhesive: Pressure-sensitive acrylic adhesive
  3. Flex: Kapton/PET Polymer film (Flexural hinge)
  4. Adhesive: Pressure-sensitive acrylic adhesive
  5. Rigid: Cardstock/Posterboard (Structural cap)

Mechanism Definition (DXF)

The design workflow began in LibreCAD. To interface correctly with our Python generation script, the `.dxf` files were structured with strict layer management:

  • Red (Cuts): Outer boundaries and release paths.
  • Blue (Hinges): Internal flexural zones (material removal).
  • Green (Mounts): Pilot holes for pin-jig alignment and motor mounts.

We designed the central spine as a modular unit, allowing us to physically swap components to test varying lengths and stiffnesses without redesigning the whole robot.

Manufacturing Sequence & Assembly

The fabrication followed a sequential process:

  1. Laser Machining: "First Pass" pattern cut into individual rigid/adhesive layers.
  2. Lamination: Five layers aligned using a pin-jig system and heat-pressed.
  3. Release: "Final Cut" pattern applied to release the structure.
  4. Folding: 2D laminates folded into 3D kinematic configurations.

Electronic Integration & Iteration

Motor Mounting: Custom mounts were 3D printed (PLA, 30% Gyroid infill). We iterated to address initial failures:

  • Stiffness: Switched to thicker flexure material to prevent buckling.
  • Protection: Implemented software torque limiting to prevent shaft shear.

The control logic is handled by an ESP32 microcontroller running MicroPython, with L298N drivers handling the power distribution.

Analysis & Results

MuJoCo Dynamic Model

We use MuJoCo as the primary environment for dynamic simulation. The model captures rigid-body dynamics, joint constraints, contact physics, and the electromechanical behavior of the actuators. The kinematic tree is structured to replicate the physical assembly: Worldbody with friction, Chassis root, Spine subtree, and Leg subassemblies.

Simulation Parameters

  • Timestep: 0.001 s (Stable RK4)
  • Integrator: RK4 (Runge-Kutta 4)
  • Friction: 0.5 (Tangential), 0.1 (Torsional)
  • Motor Gain ($k_v$): 5.0 (Calibrated to Maxon motors)

Parameter Optimization

A central goal was to determine the optimal spine configuration. We performed a parametric sweep on the Inner Shaft Size ($S_{inner}$) vector $(x,y,z)$ to maximize gait stability (minimize yaw oscillation, maximize forward velocity).

Optimization Results

  • Thickness: 2.0 mm (Resist vertical bending)
  • Length: 50.0 mm (Optimal balance of stability vs turning inertia)
  • Width: 30.0 mm (Lateral stiffness)

Using $L=50$mm, the simulation produced a stable, repeatable gait with minimal "fishtailing", unlike the unstable $L=90$mm case.

Experimental Validation

Experiments were conducted on a smooth whiteboard surface using overhead video tracking. We compared the physics model predictions against the physical reality.

Results: Sim-to-Real Gap

Contrary to the simulation predictions (stable walking), the physical prototype exhibited "Trajectory Churning":

  • Net Displacement: $\approx 0$. The robot oscillated within a 10cm bounding box.
  • Heading Drift: Severe yaw drift ($>40^\circ$ over 18s).
  • Roll Instability: Oscillations between $\pm 20^\circ$.

The failure was not just quantitative (slower speed) but qualitative (mode switch from walking to slipping).

Scientific Analysis of Divergence

1. Friction Cone Violation

The simulation assumed a friction coefficient of $\mu=0.5$ (Rubber on concrete). The Cardstock-on-Whiteboard interface had $\mu \approx 0.15$. Consequently, the leg propulsion force $F_p$ exceeded the static friction limit $\mu N$, causing the feet to slip backward instead of propelling the body forward.

2. Parasitic Compliance

The Cardstock legs, while stiff in tension, exhibited out-of-plane buckling under load. This "parasitic" compliance reduced the effective step height, causing the swing legs to drag (seen in the Z-axis data) rather than clear the ground.

3. Unmodeled Asymmetry

The simulation assumed a perfectly symmetric spine. In reality, minor manufacturing variances in the hand-folded Kapton flexures created an asymmetric restoring torque, leading to the continuous yaw drift observed.

Conclusion & Future Work

This work successfully validated the efficacy of a Cross-Axis Flexure spine in stabilizing a quadrupedal Jansen-walker in simulation, establishing a clear link between spine length and gait stability. However, the physical implementation highlighted critical constraints in SCM robotics.

To close the Sim-to-Real gap, future iterations must:

  1. Increase Friction: Add silicone/rubber pads to leg tips to satisfy the friction cone.
  2. Stiffen Links: Transition to FR4 or Carbon Fiber to eliminate parasitic buckling.
  3. Closed-Loop Control: Integrate IMU feedback to actively correct for spinal asymmetry.

Project Assignment 01: Laminate Parallel Articulated Spine

A Laminate Parallel Articulated Spine for a Cat-Scale Quadruped Robot

Jeevan Hebbal Manjunath • Varun Karthik • Yeshwanth Reddy Gurreddy

Download Report (PDF)

Abstract

This project presents the design and physical prototyping of a bio-inspired mechanism intended to serve as the articulated spine of a small quadruped robot with Jansen mechanisms for its four legs. Taking inspiration from the classical Sarrus linkage, we develop a modified planar parallel linkage that connects two rigid 3D-printed chassis plates and is intended to provide vertical support while allowing controlled in-plane bending. The spine is realized in a Smart Composite Microstructures (SCM) format using a five-layer laminate (rigid--adhesive--flexure--adhesive--rigid). Stiff outer layers form the links, while the flexure layer creates passive spring-like joints that generate a restoring torque as the spine bends. The overall morphology is scaled to roughly match the proportions of a juvenile cat while operating at a much lower mass of about 250g. We construct both a paper analogue of the mechanism and a laminate prototype integrated into a quadruped chassis. A planar kinematic model, implemented in Python, approximates the six-link Sarrus-based spine as an equivalent three-joint serial chain in the sagittal plane. Using this model, we compute representative joint torques and joint velocities for expected ground reaction forces and vertical motions of the front chassis. The results demonstrate that a linkage-based, passively compliant spine is feasible within the constraints of laminate fabrication and provides a structured way to tune the directional stiffness of the robot torso for future integration with Jansen-leg quadruped locomotion.

1. Introduction

Bio-inspired legged robots often borrow structural ideas from animals to improve stability and maneuverability. Many existing designs use rigid torsos or uniformly flexible backbones, which limit force transmission.

In contrast, we explore a mechanical articulated spine for a small quadruped robot. The design is inspired by the classical Sarrus linkage but modified into a planar parallel structure connecting two rigid chassis plates.

1.1 Project Rationale

This project isolates the spine as the central design element:

  • Parallel Articulated Spine: High vertical stiffness with controlled in-plane bending.
  • 5-Layer Laminate: Stiff links with compliant flexure joints.
  • Jansen Compatibility: Designed for planar leg mechanisms.

2. Bio-Inspiration

Hybrid Concept: Physically scaled to a juvenile cat (Body Plan) but mechnically supervised by myriapod-style robots (Sarrus-linkage backbone).

This hybrid system resembles a mammal externally but functions like a segmented arthropod internally, allowing the torso to store energy and extend stride length.

2.2 Scale Selection

Quantity Juvenile Cat Robot Target
Mass 1.5 - 2.0 kg 0.25 kg
Body Length 0.25 - 0.30 m 0.25 m
Walking Speed 0.4 - 0.6 m/s 0.2 - 0.3 m/s

3. Robot Specifications

The key physics-based parameters combine biological scaling with conservative design choices for the laminate process.

Mass & Forces: We target a total mass of 0.25kg, resulting in a body weight of ~2.45N and peak ground reaction forces of 3-5N.

Materials: The SCM process uses FR4 for rigid links (15-25 GPa) and Kapton for flexures (2.5-3.0 GPa).

Parameter Value
Peak Vertical Accel ($a_z$) ~6.2 $m/s^2$
Torsional Stiffness ($k_{\theta}$) ~1.4 Nm/rad
Max Spine Pitch ~0.17 rad

4. Physical Prototypes

To develop intuition, we first built a paper mechanism using slit-and-fold joints. The colored prismatic frames represent the front and rear body segments.

This validates the qualitative Sarrus-like motion before moving to the final 5-layer FR4 laminate construction.

5. Geometry & Kinematics

The spine is a six-link Sarrus mechanism approximated as a planar 3R serial chain in Python.

6. Performance Metrics

Peak Vertical Acceleration

$$ a_z \approx \frac{4.0 - 2.45}{0.25} \approx 6.2 \text{ m/s}^2 $$

Target Torsional Stiffness

$$ k_{\theta} \approx \frac{0.25}{0.174} \approx 1.4 \text{ Nm/rad} $$

7. Kinematic Model

Forward Kinematics: The endpoint is the vector sum of three links:

$$ P_3 = P_0 + \sum l_i [\cos\alpha_i, \sin\alpha_i]^T $$

Force Mapping

Static Analysis: We map endpoint forces to joint torques:

$$ \boldsymbol{\tau} = J^T \mathbf{F}_{ee} $$

A 4N downward load results in torques within the range of hobby servos.

8. Discussion

The planar 3R model approximates body-level forces well. Future work includes extending to 3D (lateral bending), integrating Jansen legs, and experimentally calibrating stiffness.

9. Conclusion

We successfully designed a laminate articulated spine producing vertical support with controlled compliance. The 5-layer stack is feasible, and the kinematics are tunable for future quadruped prototypes.
